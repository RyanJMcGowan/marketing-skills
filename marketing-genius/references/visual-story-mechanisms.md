# Make the creative idea visible, audible, and repeatable

The case comparison in [campaign-atlas.md](campaign-atlas.md) suggests **coherent contrast**, not a universal art style. Volkswagen used a tiny subject in blank space; Braniff changed the color of actual aircraft and uniforms; Apple staged filmic conflict; Got Milk? filled a room with plot-relevant props; Cadbury made a sustained purple performance; Budweiser made the brand name an auditory puzzle. These are inferences from selected successful cases, vulnerable to selection bias. Use them as design questions, not laws.

## Visual and sensory operations

| Operation | How to use it | Failure check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Show the missing thing** | Remove the product at the moment it matters and let the consequence unfold (*Got Milk?*). | Does the audience identify what was missing without an explanatory lecture? |
| **Make the category's hidden rule visible** | Put its normal imagery beside an honest opposite (VW's small car; Liquid Death's water-as-metal beverage). | Is the inversion an actual benefit or a costume? |
| **Embody an abstract promise** | Convert a belief into a person, scene, or action (Walt Stack running; Apple's hammer throw). | Is the metaphor culturally legible and deserved by the brand? |
| **Change reality** | Alter packaging, service, or physical environment so it advertises itself (Braniff, Inglorious Fruits, Oatly). | Can the operation deliver the advertised experience repeatedly? |
| **Create an inference gap** | Show enough for viewers to predict or solve the next beat (Bud/Weis/Er; a radio question and full mouth). | Will the payoff arrive within the actual attention window, and does it retrieve the brand? |
| **Make the ad feel like the benefit** | Give an experience analogous to the product outcome (Cadbury's pleasure). | Is there adequate category/brand learning, or merely unrelated entertainment? |
| **Turn identity into a character system** | Give product-shaped figures motives, contradictions, and relationships (M&M's). | Can characters generate new stories and still teach the product? |

### Compose by hierarchy, not volume

Start with the **semantic center**: the smallest thing that carries the contradiction. Decide whether it needs isolation, context, or escalation. Control scale, empty space, color, gaze, framing, sequence, and duration to make that center readable. Big type and fast cuts cannot rescue a vague premise. Quiet is powerful when an audience has enough context to infer what is happening; maximalism is powerful when each element belongs to the same world.

Sketch a frame with no logo or copy, then say what an unfamiliar person can observe, infer, and feel. Add the brand cue back and ask whether it resolves the image rather than merely signing it. For audio, mute the film and then listen without picture: identify what each track contributes. Silence, repetition, a distinctive voice, and a familiar song can serve the causal story; none is inherently effective. Do not use unlicensed music or another campaign's distinctive character as a shortcut.

### Engineer a small story

Write five beats, even for a still image: **normal world → disruption → audience question → consequence/reversal → brand-resolved meaning**. The beats can be simultaneous in print, or unfold in time. The audience should participate in a manageable inference rather than receive all meaning as narration. Vary the delay: early brand presence can build familiarity; a late reveal can reward attention; a late reveal that makes people remember the stunt and forget the brand is a failure.

Humor can emerge from incongruity, repeated behavior, status reversal, and committed character logic. Choose the comic target carefully. A caring brand can joke about an absurd logistical predicament without making the vulnerable person the butt of it. A celebrity or mascot is useful when their character changes the story's causal structure, not merely when their face attracts a glance.

### Build a platform rather than a single trick

Define what stays constant: a customer tension, point of view, sensory cue, character relationship, or behavioral ritual. Define what changes: setting, stakes, supporting cast, occasion, or reversal. Propose three subsequent executions that have different plots and still communicate the same brand promise. If the only variation is repeating a catchphrase, the platform is exhausted already.

Check **ownability** in context: a cue can be common in the world but uniquely associated with a brand through use. Do not casually discard existing familiar cues. Conversely, a new mascot does not automatically create recognition; it requires consistent use, reach, time, and a reason to belong.

## Image-reading worksheet for future research

When studying an ad, write observations before interpretation:

1. Literal inventory: subject, setting, color, objects, words, scale, spatial relationships, sound, motion, duration.
2. Expected category image and the precise deviation. What is surprisingly *absent*?
3. First question the viewer is likely to ask; moment of resolution; emotional movement.
4. Where the brand appears, how strongly it is linked to the resolution, and what could be recalled a day later.
5. Whether the ad shows a product truth, a fabricated metaphor, or a changed product/service reality.
6. Production, client, and distribution decisions that allowed the idea to survive.
7. Alternative explanations for the reported results; audience or cultural costs of the execution.

Do not infer a maker's thought process from the final image alone. Compare the image with primary interviews, drafts, briefs, and contemporaneous records. The most useful historical lesson is often a rejected idea or the client's decisive intervention.
