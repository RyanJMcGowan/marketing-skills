# Human stakes before product explanations

Use these firsthand accounts as questions for new briefs, not as formulas or borrowed executions. A practitioner's recollection explains a creative decision; it does not prove the campaign's independent sales effect.

## Three useful lessons

1. **Jeff Goodby and Rich Silverstein, with planner Jon Steel: notice absence in an ordinary ritual.** Goodby recounts Steel hearing a participant say that she noticed milk when it ran out. The team turned the inconvenience of absence into the premise of “got milk?” instead of repeating milk's nutritional benefits. [Goodby and Silverstein's account](https://www.masterclass.com/classes/jeff-goodby-and-rich-silverstein-teach-advertising-and-creativity/chapters/got-milk). **Ask:** At which exact moment would someone miss the product's effect, and what were they trying to do then? Do not assume every category needs a deprivation story.

2. **Mary Wells Lawrence: make the audience feel the product's consequence.** She describes changing Braniff's gray planes and cabins into a colorful experience, so the service itself carried the idea. Discussing Alka-Seltzer, she contrasts funny work with humor that leaves the audience anticipating relief. [Wells Lawrence interview](https://freshairarchive.org/segments/advertising-great-mary-wells-lawrence). **Ask:** What feeling should the scene leave behind, and can the company deliver that feeling through the actual service? A good joke or beautiful image can fail if it never arrives at the human consequence of the product.

3. **John Hegarty: use emotional understanding in service of truth.** Hegarty connects sustained persuasion with understanding what motivates people; he also argues that sometimes the product must change. His Levi's example dramatizes the real effort people once made to get the jeans they wanted. [Hegarty interview](https://radioideaxme.com/2020/09/30/john-hegarty-the-power-of-creativity/). **Ask:** What does the person already care about, what effort or compromise do they make now, and what truthful product change removes it?

## Scene-to-line exercise

For each territory, write five short sentences before any tagline:

1. A particular person is trying to do something that matters to them.
2. A familiar friction interrupts, exposes, or diminishes that moment.
3. Another person witnesses or is affected by the friction, if relevant.
4. The product changes an observable action, attention, choice, or exchange.
5. Name what has been protected, restored, or made possible in human language.

Then try the scene without the product. If the same emotional ending remains, the brand has no necessary role. Try the line without the scene. If it sounds like a feature, procedure, or inspirational abstraction, rewrite it. A brief comic observation, ambition, or sense of competence can be the human value; do not force vulnerability or sentimentality.
