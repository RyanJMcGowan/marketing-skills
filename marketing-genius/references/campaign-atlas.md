# Campaign atlas: study mechanisms, not costumes

This is a set of comparative cases, not a hall of fame or proof that any single ad caused a company's growth. Treat the credited people as collaborators, firsthand recollections as fallible, and reported business results as observational unless a credible comparison establishes incrementality. When using a precedent, inspect the original ad in its medium and the category work surrounding it. Transfer the *question it asks*, not its recognizable execution.

## Index

- Product disadvantage/absence: Volkswagen, Got Milk?, Alka-Seltzer
- Tangible brand world: Braniff, Sony Bravia, Inglorious Fruits, Liquid Death, Oatly
- Sound and serial characters: Budweiser Frogs, M&M's, Aflac
- Identity and allegory: Nike, Apple 1984, Apple Think Different
- Entertainment and cultural speed: Cadbury Gorilla, Maximum Effort, Dollar Shave Club
- Countercases: Aflac comprehension, Pepsi Jenner/Refresh, Tropicana, Liquid Death crowdfunding

## Product disadvantage and absence

### Volkswagen: Think Small and Lemon (1959–60)

**Original form.** A small, slightly angled Beetle sits high in a vast white print page above the blunt “Think Small.” The page makes smallness physically felt amid ornate, large-car advertising. “Lemon” puts a single apparently damning word next to the car, then resolves it with quality-control copy about a rejected vehicle. The reversal depends on a real product claim.

**How it emerged.** Julian Koenig originally wrote “Think Small” for a different corporate ad with three large American cars. Art director Helmut Krone preferred another headline; client Helmut Schmidt spotted the phrase in the copy, and Krone found the final visual. Bill Bernbach led the agency, but the recognizable page was not a solitary flash from him. Later versions and copy involved further collaborators. [Koenig's account in a book excerpt](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/industrynews/tip-sheet/article/50913-excerpt-think-small-basic-ugly-economical-and-foreign.html); [Bernbach's 1969 interview as republished from DDB News](https://chuukyuu.hatenablog.com/entry/20091111/1257878114); [original Lemon print reproduction](https://www.adpatina.com/products/1960-volkswagen-vw-beetle-lemon-advertisement).

**Transfer question.** Which category disadvantage can be honestly reframed into a reason to choose the product, and can the page itself prove the reframe? Historical influence does not isolate the ad's sales effect.

### Got Milk?: Aaron Burr (1993)

**Original form.** A history obsessive's room establishes his answer to a radio quiz. A peanut-butter bite, ringing phone, empty milk carton, muffled attempted answer, and lost prize create a chain of causes. The closing two-word question identifies precisely what was missing. The warehouse objects and radio sound are plot, not decoration.

**How it emerged.** Client Jeff Manning pushed beyond familiar nutrition claims; planner Jon Steel's consumer work exposed the felt loss when milk was unavailable. Jeff Goodby named the “got milk?” proposition; Rich Silverstein staged a real office milk-deprivation experiment. Tara Winkler, art director Erich Joiner, director Michael Bay, performers, and the client shaped the final work. The first execution was one of several. [ANA Educational Foundation case](https://aef.com/classroom-resources/case-histories/got-milk/); [firsthand oral history and original spot](https://musebyclios.com/advertising/inside-got-milk-and-its-dark-comedy-of-deprivation/); [team oral history](https://www.fastcompany.com/40556502/got-milk-how-the-iconic-campaign-came-to-be-25-years-ago).

**Transfer question.** When is the product's *absence* more persuasive than its presence? California volume rose from 740 million to 755 million gallons in 1993–94 in the case history, alongside a large campaign budget and other factors; do not assign the change to one film. Manning judged later “Town Without Milk” less personally felt despite its cleverness.

### Alka-Seltzer: relief through comic behavior

**Original form.** The double “plop” sonically ties the remembered line to using two tablets. “I Can't Believe I Ate the Whole Thing” repeats a husband's regret and his spouse's correction before the product and relief claim arrive; the callback makes the scene quotable without losing the solution.

**How it emerged.** A staff chemist's two-tablet recommendation preceded the jingle. Howie Cohen and art director Bob Pasqualina conceived the 1972 overindulgence spot; Mary Wells Lawrence led and defended the work at Wells Rich Greene. Wells later contrasted it with funny Alka-Seltzer work she felt did not leave people expecting relief. That is her retrospective judgment, not a controlled comparison. [Wells's interview with commercial transcript](https://freshairarchive.org/segments/advertising-great-mary-wells-lawrence); [Cohen's participant account](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/i-cant-believe-im-still-working-147295045.html).

**Transfer question.** Does the joke itself move the audience toward the product consequence, or does the brand merely sponsor the joke?

## Tangible brand worlds

### Braniff: The End of the Plain Plane (1965)

**Original form.** Colorful jets, cabin interiors, and uniforms changed the travel experience itself. Advertising gathered the crew atop a vividly colored plane and announced an end to the “plain plane.” The maximalism was coherent because the product had actually become visually different.

**How it emerged.** Mary Wells observed gray terminals and asked why flying could not feel colorful. Alexander Girard designed aircraft/interiors; Emilio Pucci designed uniforms; Charlie Moss and Phil Parker shaped the ad; client Harding Lawrence made the operational change possible. A discarded visual was recovered and developed. [Wells's firsthand memoir excerpt and linked spots](https://www.randomhouse.com/knopf/authors/lawrence/desktopnew.html); [Fresh Air interview](https://freshairarchive.org/segments/advertising-great-mary-wells-lawrence); [Braniff timeline](https://www.braniffinternational.com/braniff-airways-company-firsts).

**Transfer question.** Can a real service or product decision become the media? Brand, fleet, routes, and operations changed together; the campaign alone did not determine the airline's financial fate.

### Sony Bravia: Balls (2005)

**Original form.** A real cascade of colored balls travels down San Francisco streets to dramatize “Colour Like No Other,” filmed with restrained, lyrical music. The city provides familiar scale; the impossible-seeming real event makes color the entire visual proposition.

**How it emerged.** Sony's feature brief shifted after consumer research found category parity; client David Patton and Fallon adopted color as a human shortcut for picture quality. Juan Cabral and Richard Flintham's short script proposed the event; the production team made it real. The shoot itself attracted public footage before broadcast. [D&AD case with client and agency accounts](https://www.dandad.org/insights/awards/sony-balls-case-study-insights).

**Transfer question.** Can a tangible demonstration make a feature a felt benefit? A beautiful event needs a legible product link; the case reports business contribution without a controlled estimate for this film alone.

### Intermarché: Inglorious Fruits and Vegetables (2014)

**Original form.** Real, misshapen produce is photographed plainly and given direct, playful names. The store actually buys and discounts food normally discarded, so the posters document a new commercial practice rather than disguise inaction.

**How it emerged.** Marcel's team sought a response to food waste that would not look like greenwashing. They selected expressive real produce, pared back art direction, and made the discounted items available. D&AD reports reach, traffic, produce sales, and sell-outs; these are campaign case claims, not isolated experimental effects. [D&AD interview and case](https://www.dandad.org/insights/awards/case-study-inglorious-fruits-social-good).

**Transfer question.** Is there a real business action that the advertising can make visible and easy to join?

### Liquid Death: category-code inversion (2018 onward)

**Original form.** Water appears in a tallboy can with heavy-metal skull codes and “Murder Your Thirst.” Early videos dramatize the absurd mismatch of menacing beverage imagery and ordinary water. The package remains a portable ad at an event.

**How it emerged.** Mike Cessario had observed performers making water look like an energy drink at concerts; the beer-code package developed later, not in one instant. Collaborators designed and edited the early work. Founder accounts describe inexpensive video and paid seeding, but the initial Indiegogo campaign failed to raise meaningful capital; subsequent attention, financing, product, and distribution all mattered. [Founder interview and early chronology](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/how-i-built-this-with-guy-raz/liquid-death-mike-cessario); [original video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EeRADNpdKD4); [founder on budget and iteration](https://www.chiefmarketer.com/liquid-death-ceo-on-building-a-provocative-brand-small-marketing-budgets-and-super-bowl-ads/).

**Transfer question.** Which borrowed category codes solve an actual social or identity problem? Attention is not a purchase, and shock is a poor transplant into a sensitive product.

### Oatly: packaging as conversation

**Original form.** Cartons and outdoor ads use oversized, self-aware text that addresses the reader and sometimes the ad itself. The product package is a writing surface; the voice carries across media more consistently than a fixed layout.

**How it emerged.** The company's prospectus describes creative work connected to product decisions and consumer relations; creative director Michael Lee explains the in-house voice and willingness to polarize. Brand growth also depended on product, supply, distribution, and capital, so the ads' independent contribution is unknown. [Oatly prospectus, especially pp. 119–121](https://www.stifel.com/prospectusfiles/PD_4489.pdf); [Lee interview](https://www.creativereview.co.uk/how-i-work-michael-lee-oatly/).

**Transfer question.** Can the packaging speak in a voice that belongs to the company? A fourth-wall joke that can run for anyone is only a writing trick.

## Sound and serial characters

### Budweiser Frogs (1995)

**Original form.** Three fairly natural frogs in a dark, familiar swamp croak fragments that gradually resolve into the brand name. Crickets and ambient frogs ground an auditory puzzle; a neon Budweiser sign confirms the solution. Retelling the gag rehearses the name.

**How it emerged.** Dave Swaine and Michael Smith proposed the idea; voice/sound maker Tom Woodard describes arranging the croaks and atmosphere from drawings and an audio track. Later frog/lizard episodes added a serial world. A legal dispute over a prior frog submission makes absolute “first ever” origin claims unwise. [Woodard's account](https://ontheavenue.net/how-tom-woodard-became-a-frog/); [original spot and litigation record](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp2/55/151/2376415/); [IPA case spanning multiple Budweiser campaigns](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/case-studies/budweiser-frogs-lizards-whassup).

**Transfer question.** Can the sound or joke require the brand name to complete it? IPA's broader results cannot isolate the first frogs spot. Beer mascots also raise an [unintended-child-audience concern](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7063999/).

### M&M's: characters as an entertainment system (mid-1990s onward)

**Original form.** Product-colored candy bodies become actors with distinct temperaments. Red's bravado and Yellow's innocence create conflict; a brief Christmas scene makes Santa and the talking candies astonish each other. Their bodies remain recognizable brand assets even when the bag is absent.

**How it emerged.** Susan Credle and Steve Rutter envisioned a cast and a writers' room, not a one-off spot. Animators and storyboard artists defined expressions and behavior. Credle says the characters could extend to entertainment and merchandise. [Credle interview](https://www.aaf.org/Public/Public/Events/Advertising-Hall-of-Fame/Inductee-Interviews/AHOF_Interview_Susan_Fowler_Credle.aspx); [animation team account](https://www.digipen.edu/showcase/news/bfa-program-director-claymation-commercial); [holiday spot making-of](https://uproxx.com/tv/mms-christmas-commercial-they-do-exist/).

**Transfer question.** Do character flaws and relationships generate new stories, and does the character itself carry product identity? Sales anecdotes do not isolate the characters' effect.

### Aflac Duck (2000 onward)

**Original form.** In an ordinary park, people discuss insurance but cannot name Aflac; a white duck quacks the answer with growing frustration. A phonetic resemblance turns an unfamiliar name into a sound people can repeat.

**How it emerged.** Eric David heard the name as a quack, Tom Amico developed the spot, and Linda Kaplan Thaler championed the odd fragment. Maker accounts report strong recall tests against another proposed approach. [Creator interview](https://www.georgiatrend.com/2010/05/01/the-duck-stops-here/); [brand history](https://www.aflac.com/about-aflac/our-company/the-aflac-duck.aspx); [company's later product-education campaign](https://newsroom.aflac.com/2019-01-07-Aflac-Launches-New-Marketing-Strategy-Featuring-What-Aflac-Isnt-to-Shed-Light-on-Everything-Aflac-Is).

**Transfer question.** Does the mnemonic teach the *category and benefit* as well as the name? Aflac later acknowledged that many people knew the name but not what its policies did.

## Identity, allegory, and emotion

### Nike: Just Do It launch (1988)

**Original form.** The first film followed 80-year-old runner Walt Stack across the Golden Gate Bridge; his matter-of-fact humor and effort gave a broad invitation to action a human face. This was not a montage of flawless elite athletes.

**How it emerged.** Dan Wieden adapted an unrelated, disturbing phrase into a short imperative that people could apply beyond sport. His account concerns the line, while the launch spot embodies it through an ordinary person. [Nike's account of the first film](https://about.nike.com/en/newsroom/releases/nike-why-do-it-campaign); [Wieden interview](https://www.designindaba.com/videos/interviews/dan-wieden-part-1-we-started-ignoring-rules-advertising).

**Transfer question.** Who can enact the belief more persuasively than a product shot? Nike's growth involved product, distribution, sponsorship, and athletes, not one line alone.

### Apple: 1984 (1984)

**Original form.** A blue-gray, obedient crowd watches an authoritarian screen; a single vivid athlete enters and smashes it. The product is absent as an object but present as the disruptive act. The end card resolves the industry allegory.

**How it emerged.** Lee Clow, Steve Hayden, Brent Thomas, Ridley Scott, Apple teams, and leadership all shaped the film. An Apple team eyewitness describes earlier print concepts, the storyboard, divided client reactions, one surviving Super Bowl placement, and news rebroadcasts. [Macintosh team history and original film](https://folklore.org/1984.html).

**Transfer question.** Is the obstacle clear enough that a decisive action can stand for the product? Expensive allegory without a true challenger position is theater; publicity and launch sales are not cleanly separable.

### Apple: Think Different (1997)

**Original form.** Black-and-white portraits of culturally recognized rule breakers contrast with a small color Apple mark. The brand borrows an identity from people, with little product imagery.

**How it emerged.** Craig Tanimoto made the initial portrait/line pairing; Rob Siltanen wrote the manifesto, with Lee Clow, Ken Segall, Steve Jobs and others refining it. Jobs initially disliked a rough script. In a contemporary presentation, Jobs tied brand clarity to changes in the product line and distribution. [Siltanen's documented firsthand account](https://www.forbes.com/sites/onmarketing/2011/12/14/the-real-story-behind-apples-think-different-campaign/); [Jobs's 1997 presentation in the Steve Jobs Archive](https://book.stevejobsarchive.com/).

**Transfer question.** Can the brand credibly serve the identity it invokes? The campaign did not alone turn Apple around.

## Entertainment and cultural speed

### Cadbury: Gorilla (2007)

**Original form.** A lifelike gorilla waits in a purple rehearsal room, close-ups drawing out anticipation, then drums to Phil Collins. No chocolate demonstration appears in the scene; the film tries to deliver the pleasure the brand wanted people to associate with eating chocolate.

**How it emerged.** Juan Cabral had conceived the gorilla performance separately; Cadbury's “make people feel good” brief supplied a use for it. Client Phil Rumbol defended the strange, long film through internal resistance. Pretests did not make it seem logically sensible but did register the intended feeling. D&AD and Rumbol report sales gains and strong returns; these are retrospective observational claims. [Cabral and Rumbol firsthand interview with original film](https://www.theguardian.com/media-network/2016/jan/07/how-we-made-cadburys-gorilla-ad); [D&AD case](https://www.dandad.org/insights/awards/cadbury-gorilla-case-study-insights).

**Transfer question.** Can the ad *be* the emotional benefit rather than describe it, while preserving sufficient brand association? This was one well-resourced, well-distributed exception, not permission for arbitrary surrealism.

### Maximum Effort: Match, Aviation, Mint, Deadpool

**Original form.** Match makes Satan and a personified 2020 a romance in emptied pandemic spaces; the dating product's matching function is the joke's structure. Other work responds quickly to a cultural moment using a consistent self-aware voice.

**How it emerged.** Ryan Reynolds credits partner George Dewey with pitching the Satan/2020 pairing and distinguishes his subsequent writing; Deadpool's constrained promotion helped establish the practice. Dewey warns that copying fourth-wall jokes misses the character's deeper motives. [Reynolds interview](https://www.washingtonpost.com/washington-post-live/2022/07/28/transcript-hollywood-new-entrepreneurial-era-conversation-with-ryan-reynolds/); [Reynolds and Dewey's collaboration](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/linkedin_limitations-are-a-wonderful-creative-tool-activity-6873614927983087616-KiIZ); [Dewey interview](https://aws1.campaignasia.com/article/inside-the-deadpool-and-wolverine-marketing-bonanza/497441).

**Transfer question.** Can the company interpret an existing conversation in its own voice, quickly and without punching down? Celebrity reach, intellectual property, ownership stakes, and cross-promotion are not portable to a small firm.

### Dollar Shave Club: Our Blades Are F***ing Great (2012)

**Original form.** Founder Michael Dubin walks through the real warehouse delivering a clear price/convenience argument while progressively absurd background acts reward attention. The deadpan tour and selling points occupy the same scene.

**How it emerged.** Dubin drew on improv experience, wrote the spot, and director Lucia Aniello shot it in a day. Maker accounts report a modest production cost, a crashed site, and early orders; later company value cannot be assigned to the film alone. [Original upload with credits](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZUG9qYTJMsI); [Dubin firsthand account](https://www.inc.com/magazine/201707/lindsay-blakely/how-i-did-it-michael-dubin-dollar-shave-club.html).

**Transfer question.** Can a real place and founder voice host a funny progression while communicating a sharp, testable business proposition?

## Countercases and cautions

- **Aflac comprehension gap:** name recognition can outrun product understanding. [Aflac's own explanation](https://newsroom.aflac.com/2019-01-07-Aflac-Launches-New-Marketing-Strategy-Featuring-What-Aflac-Isnt-to-Shed-Light-on-Everything-Aflac-Is).
- **Pepsi Jenner (2017):** the brand borrowed protest imagery without earning its role. Pepsi [launched](https://www.pepsico.com/newsroom/press-releases/2017/pepsi-debuts-moments-campaign-starring-kendall-jenner) and [withdrew/apologized for](https://www.pepsico.com/newsroom/press-releases/2017/pepsi-statement-re--pepsi-moments-content) the film; attention was a liability.
- **Pepsi Refresh:** participation and grants were substantial, but a [Harvard Business School case](https://store.hbr.org/product/the-pepsi-refresh-project-a-thirst-for-change/512018) questions the commercial return. Do not assign contemporaneous cola declines to the program without a counterfactual.
- **Tropicana package redesign:** losing familiar recognition cues can confuse buyers even if the new design is tidy. [Ehrenberg-Bass discussion](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/brands-of-distinction); do not treat every asset as sacred, but measure its fame and uniqueness before removal.
- **Liquid Death's failed crowdfunding:** a strong video did not initially generate enough pledged demand. [Founder chronology](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/how-i-built-this-with-guy-raz/liquid-death-mike-cessario).

Across these examples the recurring *inference* is a familiar expectation disrupted by one causal contradiction, then resolved through the product or brand's role. The visual styles range from blank paper to total sensory spectacle. A memorable image is a compact explanation or experience of the idea, not a mandatory minimalist look. Production, client trust, distribution, and a working business remain part of the result.
