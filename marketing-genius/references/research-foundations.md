# Research foundations, evidence grades, and experiments

The skill's matrix is a practical synthesis of cognitive and advertising research plus campaign comparisons. It is **not** a validated predictor of creative success. Different tasks call for different degrees of novelty, explicitness, emotion, and brand exposure. Treat any effect from a laboratory or selected archive as context-dependent.

## What research supports

| Lens | Useful finding and boundary | Primary source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Generate, then explore** | Creative cognition can be modeled as generating preinventive structures and interpreting/developing them. This supports separating divergent idea making from judgment; it does not specify a winning ad. | Finke, Ward & Smith, *Creative Cognition* (MIT Press, 1992/1996): https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262560962/creative-cognition/ |
| **Conceptual blending** | Selective projection from different mental spaces can yield emergent meaning. Transfer an underlying causal relation, not a random juxtaposition. | Fauconnier & Turner, *Cognitive Science* 22(2), 1998: https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2202_1 |
| **Divergence and convergence** | An advertising experiment found technique effects differed among creatives, account executives, and students. Do not prescribe one universal brainstorming ritual. | Kilgour & Koslow, *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* 37, 2009: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-009-0133-5 |
| **Originality with fit** | An advertising meta-analysis treats creativity as originality plus appropriateness; benefits were clearer for attitudes than for brand memory and direct sales evidence remained limited. Novelty is not a substitute for relevance. | Rosengren et al., *Journal of Marketing* 84(6), 2020: https://doi.org/10.1177/0022242920929288 |
| **Original yet familiar** | Eye-tracking research found ads combining originality and familiarity drew the most attention to the brand and improved subsequent brand memory. A familiar brand/category cue can help an odd execution resolve correctly. | Pieters, Warlop & Wedel, *Management Science* 48(6), 2002: https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.48.6.765.192 |
| **Graspable incongruity** | Experiments found moderate ad–brand incongruity sometimes outperformed both congruity and extreme incongruity; weak brand knowledge or limited processing can change the effect. Do not maximize strangeness. | *Journal of Advertising* 43(4), 2014: https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2014.891087; *International Journal of Advertising* 36(3), 2017: https://doi.org/10.1080/02650487.2016.1169579 |
| **Visual rhetoric and culture** | Visual metaphors may require cultural knowledge to be understood; effects in experiments weakened or vanished for viewers lacking it. Test the intended audience's interpretation rather than assuming the clever visual is universal. | McQuarrie & Mick, *Journal of Consumer Research* 26(1), 1999: https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1086/209549 |
| **Narrative persuasion** | Transportation in a story can affect beliefs and evaluations. This supports making a product consequence felt, while warning that vivid stories can also persuade without adequate truth. | Green & Brock, *JPSP* 79(5), 2000: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701; Escalas, *Journal of Consumer Research* 33(4), 2007: https://doi.org/10.1086/510216 |
| **Humor's unequal effects** | A meta-analysis found humor's lift on ad liking larger than its effect on brand attitude, with context and credibility tradeoffs. Measure what is remembered about the product. | Eisend, *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* 37, 2009: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-008-0096-y |
| **Sharing is its own outcome** | Research on New York Times sharing linked high-arousal emotion with greater transmission. This is not a finding that virality predicts purchases. | Berger & Milkman, *Journal of Marketing Research* 49(2), 2012: https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353 |

Check original papers before stating a numerical effect; abstracts and practitioner summaries can omit crucial moderators. A sensory pattern observed in a dozen selected iconic ads is a hypothesis, not a population-wide law.

## Evidence ladder for campaign claims

Keep these outcomes separate in a case study or a pitch:

1. **Artifact and process:** original ad, dated brief, drafts, creator and client recollections. Recollections can be self-serving or contradictory; name the team and uncertainty.
2. **Exposure and attention:** distribution, views, press, social sharing, awards, pretest response. These show reach or reaction, not necessarily correct learning.
3. **Brand learning:** unaided brand/category attribution, meaning, delayed retrieval, competitor confusion, and distinctive-cue recognition.
4. **Behavior and economics:** leads, trials, sales, margin, retention, incremental profit, and acquisition cost relative to an appropriate comparison.
5. **Causality:** a credible holdout or design that separates the campaign from product, price, distribution, seasonality, publicity, and competitors. Most famous-campaign histories do not reach this rung.

The IPA's [*Crisis in Creative Effectiveness*](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/publications-reports/the-crisis-in-creative-effectiveness) questions treating creative awards as commercial proof, though its effectiveness-case sample is selected. A study of [25 large retail advertising experiments](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/130/4/1941/1914592) found advertising ROI difficult to estimate precisely even with substantial spend. Do not promise that a small A/B test can certify a home run.

## A practical, low-cost diagnostic sequence

Use these as questions and observations, not as a standardized psychometric instrument:

1. **Before production:** Ask several target customers to recount the real occasion and language; inspect competitor ads and existing brand cues. State the behavioral aim and strongest alternative explanation for success.
2. **Rough concept exposure:** Show a sketch, read a script, or play an animatic in a realistic cluttered context. Ask without prompts: “What happened?”, “How did it make you feel?”, “Who was it for?”, “What do they offer?” Avoid leading brand questions first.
3. **Delayed retrieval:** After 24–48 hours, ask what they remember, which brand, what it does, and which distinctive element they recall. Wrong-brand attribution is a serious failure even if the story is loved.
4. **Compare alternatives:** Include an ordinary category control and a different creative territory; check whether oddness helps correct brand memory or merely earns attention. Do not let a tiny convenience sample make a quantitative sales claim.
5. **Small real-world test:** If feasible, randomize exposure or use a comparable holdout for a defined action such as qualified visits or trial. Track reach, production cost, repeatability, and confounders. Treat inconclusive results as inconclusive.

Pretesting may favor a familiar, easily paraphrased idea over a strange but emotionally resonant one. A confused first account is a diagnostic: determine whether the intended feeling and brand resolution land after the reveal. Do not change the central idea solely to optimize instant stated liking, and do not shield an incomprehensible concept from evidence by calling all criticism a failure of research.

## Beyond an ad

Exceptional work often required a client to support the idea, a product or service that delivered it, repeated exposure, distribution, and collaborators who refined a strange fragment. [D&AD's interviews with clients and makers](https://www.dandad.org/insights/awards/cadbury-gorilla-case-study-insights) show how a left-field concept survived disagreement; this is process evidence, not a universal causal estimate. In a campaign plan, articulate the operational change, rights, approvals, cost, and next three executions that let the idea remain true after launch.
